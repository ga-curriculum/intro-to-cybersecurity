## Common threats in the wild

![Threats like malware, social engineering, and network attacks.](./assets/threats.png)

There are multiple types of cyberattacks, and the list is constantly evolving. As older vulnerabilities become less effective, bad actors move on to exploit new social and technical weak spots. Here are a few of the typical security issues seen in the wild.

### Malware

Malware includes many different varieties of software exploits. Criminals use malware to steal data, disrupt operations, and gain access to systems they wouldn't be able to otherwise. Here is a list of different types and a summary of what they do:

- **Ransomware**: Designed to lock users out of systems or data until a ransom is paid.
- **Spyware**: Spies on user activity in an attempt to gather valuable information from a user's day-to-day tasks.
- **Viruses**: Viruses spread through files and require an initial user action of some kind to infect a system. This type of malware can have various end goals and is often held in trojans.
- **Trojans**: Software or files that pretend to be legitimate in order to deliver a malware payload like ransomware, spyware, or viruses. Trojans are used to manipulate users into taking actions they might not usually take.
- **Worms**: Designed to exploit vulnerabilities automatically. These self-propagate by taking advantage of network protocols or operating system weaknesses. They're able to spread between devices quickly and can be massively dangerous.

### Social engineering

In short, social engineering exploits the vulnerabilities of humans with the end goal of stealing information or valuables. It can also be used to penetrate systems.

Social engineering doesn't require deep technical knowledge but can be lucrative, making it one of the most common threats. By its nature, social engineering often preys on the socially disadvantaged. However, high-value targets with broad access to an organization's systems are the ultimate prize.

Here is a list of common tactics you should be aware of:

- **Phishing**: Uses fake emails or login pages to get login information.
- **Vishing**/**Smishing**: Similar to phishing, except they are carried out over phone calls (vishing) or text (smishing) to access information or assets.
- **Pretexting**: A scenario used to convince a victim to trust an attacker. Put another way, it's a lie that aligns with a person's expectations. Pretexting is often a component of all the above tactics.
- **Baiting**: Uses enticing offers to lure victims. The bait can be anything - a USB drive that appears to have been discarded or a free digital download of a movie. This is also a common tactic used alongside phishing, vishing, and smishing.

### Network attacks

As you might assume, network attacks exploit vulnerabilities in networks and systems. While there are many network-focused attacks, the most common ones are listed below:

- **Man-in-the-middle**: Involves intercepting and manipulating messages between two parties that trust each other. This may be used to inject malware into otherwise trusted communication channels. A variety of this is a passive **eavesdropping** attack, where communications are intercepted and read but not altered.
- **Distributed denial-of-service (DDoS)**: A group of coordinated attackers that send a massive amount of network traffic to a single targeted system or service. Attackers typically use *botnets* to carry out these attacks, amplifying the amount of traffic they are able to generate. *Botnets* are a collection of coordinated systems that have been infected with malware.
- **Brute force attacks**: Attacking a system by rapidly trying different password combinations to gain access.
- **Evil twins**: Fake Wi-Fi networks owned by attackers that impersonate real ones. These often enable eavesdropping or man-in-the-middle attacks. These are also referred to as rogue access points.

Let's look at some scenarios and discuss the attack vectors used to carry out an attack.

### Scenario 1: The coffee shop trap

Maria is working at a coffee shop and needs to check her bank account. She connects to "Free_Coffee_WiFi" without checking if it's legitimate. A hacker created this network to steal information!

Let's discuss:

- What type of attack is this?
- What did Maria do wrong?
- What actions could have Maria taken to avoid falling victim to this attack?

When using technology in public, be extra careful! These might not be things everyone thinks about, but a bad actor will! Taking precautions can prevent malware from reaching your system.

### Scenario 2: The email oops

John received an urgent email from his "boss" asking him to buy gift cards for a client. He rushed to help... but it wasn't really his boss!

Let's discuss:

- What type of attack is this?
- What did John do wrong?
- What red flags could John have noticed to help prevent this attack?

This is the perfect example of a social engineering attack. Be careful when taking requests at work, and ensure they're actually coming from the parties you think they are! It's okay to ask questions, it's better to be safe than sorry.

**Remember**: Security is a journey, not a destination. Keep learning, and stay safe!
